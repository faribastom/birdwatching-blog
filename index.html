May 14 Yellow Warbler Sourwood Creek Trail, 6:12 AM

The morning the warblers finally arrived

Migration had been quiet for two weeks — a few kinglets, one unconvincing flicker — and then, overnight, the understory filled with yellow. Not a slow trickle either. A fallout, the kind birders wait years for, where a cold front pins a whole wave of neotropical migrants to the first patch of trees they find.

Read the full entry

I nearly didn't go. Rain overnight, low cloud, the kind of gray morning that usually means an empty trail and a wasted thermos of coffee. But the wind had swung north the day before, and a north wind in mid-May after a warm stretch is the single most reliable predictor of a fallout — migrating birds riding a tailwind south suddenly hit a headwind and drop wherever they can.

They dropped at Sourwood Creek. Yellow Warblers first, a dozen of them working the willows at eye level, unbothered by how close I'd gotten. Then a single Blackburnian, its throat so orange it looked lit from inside. A Canada Warbler wearing what field guides insist on calling a "necklace," which undersells it — more like a strand of ink flicked across a gray chest.

The birds that stop are always more interesting than the birds that don't have to. Fallout conditions force migrants into habitat they wouldn't normally choose, which is exactly why you see species you'd otherwise miss.

By eight the fog burned off, the wind eased, and most of them lifted out within the hour, gone north before I'd finished the coffee. That's the trade with a fallout morning: extraordinary, and gone by breakfast.

April 30 Wood Thrush Backyard, dusk

What the wood thrush is actually doing with its throat

The wood thrush's song sounds like two flutes played slightly out of sync with each other — and that's almost exactly what's happening. Here's the anatomy behind the most underrated sound in a Maryland backyard.

Read the full entry

Songbirds don't have a larynx like we do. Instead they have a syrinx, positioned lower in the throat where the trachea splits into the two bronchial tubes leading to the lungs. Because it sits at that fork, a syrinx can, in some species, control both branches independently — which means one bird can produce two notes at once, from two separate sources of air, and layer them into a single call.

The wood thrush is one of the best examples of this. Its song ends in that liquid, bell-like flourish — often transcribed as "ee-oh-lay" — where the second half is actually a harmony the bird is singing against itself. Slow a recording down and the two voices separate cleanly, each holding its own pitch.

None of that explains why it's the sound that empties out a whole day of noise. It shows up right at dusk, when the yard has gone the color of the underside of a leaf, and it does that every single evening from late April until the young fledge. I've stopped trying to explain that part.

April 9 Great Blue Heron Millrace Pond

Learning to stand still for twenty minutes

The best birding advice I've ever gotten had nothing to do with optics. It was: stop walking. A heron taught me that, mostly by making me feel ridiculous.

Read the full entry

Most new birders — myself very much included — walk a trail the way you'd walk anywhere else: at a normal human pace, scanning as you go. It works fine for spotting motion, and it's almost useless for spotting patience, which is the entire hunting strategy of a Great Blue Heron.

A heron will stand in six inches of water for twenty minutes without a visible twitch, waiting for a fish to forget it's there. Walk past at a normal pace and you will see a gray shape that might be a stump. Stop, and stay stopped, for the same twenty minutes, and you start to see the whole pond: the muskrat working the far bank, a kingfisher rattling in on schedule every four minutes, the heron's neck uncoiling in one motion that ends with a fish already swallowed.

I've started timing myself. Twenty minutes, one spot, no phone. It's the single change that improved my birding more than any binocular upgrade did, and it was free.
